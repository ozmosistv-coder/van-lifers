Vanlife Guide — UK, Ireland & Europe

370+ verified campsites, aires, stellplatz and wild camping spots — plus the context on Europe's 23,000+ campsites, the apps that find them, and the practical rules you need to know before you park.

In the UK, “wild camping” in a vehicle is not legal in England, Wales, or Northern Ireland without explicit landowner permission. Even in Scotland, where the Outdoor Access Code permits wild camping on foot, this right does not extend to motorhomes or campervans — you still need permission or a designated spot.

Across Europe the picture is more varied. France, Germany, Italy and Spain all have networks of aires and official motorhome stopovers, while wild-camping rules differ hugely country to country — and are often stricter than people assume. Always check local rules and signage before you park.

Legal stopover options include: campsites, Brit Stops (pub/farm networks), Certificated Locations (CLs), pub stopovers, European aires (France, Italy, Spain, Portugal), Stellplatz (Germany, Austria, Switzerland), and community apps like Park4Night and Campercontact — always check recent reviews before you commit.

🌿 Wild Camping in Europe — Know the Rules First

Wild camping rules vary enormously. Scotland, Norway, Sweden and Finland are the most permissive; Germany, Italy, England and Wales are not. Always check before you pitch.

Important: Wild camping is illegal in Germany, Italy, England and Wales without landowner permission. Some countries (such as France) have loopholes where you can bivvy but not pitch a tent. Norway, Sweden and Finland allow wild camping under allemansrätten (everyman’s right) — typically two nights on uncultivated land, at least 150 metres from habitation. Scotland allows it under the Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003, but follow the Scottish Outdoor Access Code. Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania generally permit wild camping away from homes and protected areas. Campervans and motorhomes are usually treated more strictly than tents — always confirm whether vehicle overnighting is allowed, not just tent camping.

💡 Vanlife Tips — UK, Ireland & Europe

  • Use the apps: Park4Night and Campercontact for the continent, SearchForSites and Brit Stops for the UK and Ireland. Community-driven, with recent reviews from other vanlifers.
  • Always ask permission: If you spot a promising pub or farm, go in, ask politely, and buy something. Respectful requests are often met with a welcoming “yes”.
  • Learn the local rules: wild-camping laws vary enormously across Europe. Fines are real, and a clamped van ruins a trip — check before you park, not after.
  • Aire etiquette: on European aires, keep noise down, use the service point for waste, and pay what's asked. Cheap stopovers survive only while they're respected.
  • Self-build reality check: insulation and electrics are the two systems that most often cause problems. Do them properly, fuse everything, and leave maintenance access.
  • Adults-only sites: book ahead, especially in school holidays. Minimum ages vary (16+, 18+, sometimes 50+), and policies on dogs and music differ. Always confirm before you drive.
  • Winter planning: many campsites close out of season — check opening dates, carry a B2B charger for short winter days, and keep an eye on mountain-pass closures.

🔧 Base Van Choice

The four most popular base vans in the UK, and what each one is best at. Choose based on how you'll use the van — not what looks best on Instagram.

Best all-rounder

Volkswagen Transporter (T5 / T6 / T6.1)

The default UK self-build. Compact enough for daily driving, fits under 2m height barriers, and has the biggest aftermarket for camper components — swivel seats, pop-tops, kitchen pods. Holds its value better than almost anything else.

SWB or LWB · ~2.0m height · huge parts support · strong resale
Best value & width

Ford Transit Custom

Often wider than the Transporter, which means a true width-ways bed in some builds — a huge space-saver. Cheaper to buy and maintain, with the most extensive UK service network. The value king of UK conversions.

SWB or LWB · wider load area · cheaper parts · strong UK dealer network
Best for space

Fiat Ducato / Peugeot Boxer / Citroën Relay

The Sevel trio — basically the same van underneath. Boxy, useful interior width, and the workhorse choice for transverse beds and space-efficient builds. Plenty of second-hand examples and strong converter support.

L2H2 or L3H2 · boxy interior · shared platform · watch condition & payload
Best for full-time

Mercedes Sprinter / VW Crafter

Vertical side walls, generous payload and a strong long-distance platform ecosystem. The premier choice for full-time off-grid living or specialist builds. Parts and purchase cost are higher, and older vans need a careful rust check.

LWB · vertical walls · high payload · premium build ecosystem
Euro 6 compliance matters. For 2026 urban touring, a Euro 6 van avoids ULEZ/LEZ charges in London, Bristol, Birmingham and beyond. Always verify the unladen weight before you build — a 3,500kg GVW limit is reached surprisingly quickly once you add insulation, furniture, water and people.

🛏️ Layout — Rock and Roll vs Fixed Bed

The two dominant UK layouts. Choose based on how you’ll use the van, not what looks best on Instagram.

Flexible

Rock and Roll Bed

A crash-tested rear seat that belts passengers by day and folds flat into a double bed at night. Gives you a social lounge layout and works well in medium or smaller vans. The default choice for most UK conversions.

Belts passengers · folds flat · suits 2+2 layouts · best for daily drivers
Comfort

Fixed Bed

A permanent bed — usually transverse or longitudinal — with storage underneath. No setup at night, more comfort, and often a better night’s sleep. Works best in a LWB van or a dedicated tourer where you don’t need rear travel seats.

No setup · more storage · suits LWB · best for full-time tourers

If you need rear belted seats, a rock-and-roll bed is the safer and more practical route. If you’re touring as a couple and want a proper mattress and a garage underneath, a fixed bed is the better long-term choice.

🧱 Insulation, Electrics & Water

The hidden systems that make or break a build. Get these right before you line the van.

Thermal

Insulation

Use closed-cell foam (PIR boards or closed-cell roll) against the metal to stop condensation and cold bridging. Avoid Rockwool or sheep’s wool — they hold moisture and can rot the van from the inside out. Sound-deadening first, then insulation, then a vapour strategy.

Closed-cell foam · PIR boards · sound deadening · avoid moisture-trapping wool
Power

12V Electrics

Plan the system on paper before buying anything. A typical UK build uses a 100–300Ah LiFePO4 leisure battery, an MPPT solar controller, a DC-DC charger for alternator charging, and an inverter only if you need 230V. Fuse every positive run close to the battery.

LiFePO4 · MPPT · DC-DC charger · fused circuits · size to consumption
Plumbing

Water System

Most builds run a 45–70L fresh tank and a separate grey waste tank. Internal fresh tanks are common, with an underslung grey tank to save space. Use food-grade hose, a 12V pump, and plan for frost protection if you tour in winter.

45–70L fresh · separate grey · 12V pump · frost protection

📋 DVLA Re-Registration Checklist

If you want the V5C body type changed to “motor caravan”, the van must meet specific internal and external criteria. Since mid-2019, DVLA re-categorisation requires the features below — and they may request an inspection.

  • Seats and table: at least one seat and a table that can be used in the living area.
  • Sleeping accommodation: a bed, which can be part of the seating, shown in the use position.
  • Cooking facilities: a fixed cooker or hob.
  • Storage: fixed storage cupboards or compartments.
  • Two or more windows on at least one side of the main body — not counting the driver or passenger door windows — to provide daylight into the living area.
  • A separate side door providing access to the living accommodation (excluding driver and passenger doors).
  • A high-top roof or pop-top elevating roof is a common expected feature.
  • Motor caravan-style graphics on both sides of the vehicle, and an awning bar attached to either side.
  • Photographic evidence: exterior photos of both sides, front and back with plates visible; interior photos of each required feature with the bed and table in the use position; VIN/chassis number and V5C.

You do not have to re-register as a motor caravan to use the van as a camper. But if you want the lower insurance band, toll classifications, or to avoid LGV parking restrictions, the V5C change is worth pursuing. Always tell your insurer about the conversion, regardless of the body type on the V5C.

🗓️ A Sensible Build Sequence

Do these in order. The expensive failures usually happen when windows, seats or appliances are bought before the use case, layout and weight are agreed.

  • Define the job of the van. One sentence: who, where, what season, how long, what non-negotiables. Rent a van for a wet weekend if you’re unsure.
  • Set the budget and delivery route. Decide what you’ll DIY, what you’ll pay a specialist for, and what your contingency is.
  • Buy and baseline the correct van. Record internal dimensions at several heights, plated gross and axle limits, and weigh it empty.
  • Draw the layout and weight plan. Mock it up with cardboard boxes before you cut anything.
  • Plan the integrated systems. Electrical, water, heating, ventilation and fuel routes must coexist.
  • Strip, repair and cut openings. Windows, vents, seats and exterior penetrations — get these done early and weather-tight.
  • Install hidden infrastructure. Insulation, cables, pipes, ducts and service access.
  • Line and build furniture. Lightweight, fixed structure with maintenance access.
  • Commission everything. Test and label every circuit and system.
  • Legal and road validation. Insurance confirmed, V5C updated if required, weights checked, shakedown trip done.

Important: Before buying a lithium battery, confirm your existing charger (solar controller, DC-DC charger, or mains charger) has a lithium/LiFePO4 charging profile. Most modern units do, but older equipment may not.

Putting it together — a typical pairing: A Victron SmartSolar MPPT 100/20 (~£95) paired with a Fogstar Drift 105Ah (~£300) creates a reliable, Bluetooth-monitored core for a couple's campervan. Add a 175W–200W solar panel and a DC-DC charger, and you have a system that will comfortably run a compressor fridge, lights, and device charging through a UK summer. For full-time winter use, step up to a self-heating battery and a 100/30 controller.
